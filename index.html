Geothermal well arrays: efficient modeling, placement and LLM decision support

Deep geothermal wells can be used to generate electricity, but modeling them is computationally expensive. My thesis looked at how to model single wells and arrays of wells efficiently, how to find good well positions within an array, and whether LLMs can support decisions in the field.

The problem

Simulating a single deep geothermal well is already costly. An array is harder still: the wells interfere with each other, so that interference has to be part of the model too.

Efficient modeling and well placement

I worked on efficient ways to model deep wells, first individually and then as arrays. Because scoring every candidate layout means running an expensive simulation, I evaluated several black-box (derivative-free) optimization methods for finding optimal well positions in an array.

LLMs for decision support

I evaluated how accurate retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is on geothermal well research, and compared models fine-tuned on a geothermal research corpus against general-purpose LLMs trained on the web.

Publications

  • Decision Support and Modeling with Large Language Models for Geothermal Arrays Stanford Geothermal Workshop, 2025

Methods

Diabetic retinopathy detection on low-quality retina images

Models for detecting diabetic retinopathy are usually trained on high-quality images. I worked on improving how well such a model performs on images taken with lower-quality cameras.

The problem

High-quality, properly labelled retina images are readily available, for example on Kaggle. But in many clinics in developing countries, access to high-end medical equipment isn't a given, and images come from lower-quality cameras. A model that only works on clinic-grade images doesn't help where screening is needed most.

Approach

In this study, the lower-quality images were also labelled, which let us measure performance on them. The settings we were aiming for are ones where those labels might not exist.

03 / Digital phenotyping

Lab
MIT D-Lab
Supervisor
Dr. Richard Fletcher
Methods
Deep learning, CAM, LIME

Predicting daily stress from phones and wearables

Phones and wearables already record a lot about how we live. This project tested whether that data can predict how stressed someone is on a given day.

The problem

We collected behavioral and physiological data from participants' phones and wearables: mobility, physical activity, how often they called or messaged, and sleep. The goal was to predict each participant's stress level, using their own reported stress as ground truth. Self-reports are biased, so we recalibrated them before using them as labels.

From sensing to prediction

Interpretability is a major challenge in ML for healthcare. A stress score is far more useful when you can see which signals influenced it.

About

About me

I'm Edwin Ouko, a software engineer at Microsoft in Seattle, where I work on Azure Local — cloud infrastructure that runs on customers' own hardware, built on Kubernetes and Linux.

At MIT (BS and MEng, EECS), my research centered on making models useful outside ideal conditions. At D-Lab with Dr. Richard Fletcher, I worked on adapting retinal disease detection to low-cost cameras common in developing-world clinics, and on predicting stress from passive phone and wearable data. In the Julia Lab, my master's thesis developed efficient models of deep geothermal well arrays and evaluated how LLMs — retrieval-augmented and domain fine-tuned — can support decisions in that field.

I still build embedded and electronics projects, and I'm always interested in applied ML that leaves the lab.
